# Vietnam Energy Transition: Policy Context

This page provides the policy and economic background that motivates the DGE-METRIC model scenarios. It draws on Vietnam's Power Development Plan 8 (PDP8), the IWH Investment Needs Assessment, and the GIZ-IWH Financial Assessment.

---

## Vietnam's energy challenge

Vietnam is one of Southeast Asia's fastest-growing economies. Energy demand has grown roughly in line with GDP over the past two decades, with electricity consumption doubling approximately every ten years. This growth has been powered largely by coal, making Vietnam a significant and rising emitter of CO₂.

At the same time, Vietnam has made international commitments that require a structural departure from this trend:

- **Net-zero by 2050** — pledged at COP26 and restated in the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC).
- **60% renewable electricity share by 2030** — under the revised Power Development Plan 8 (PDP8, Decision 500/QD-TTg, 2023).
- **No new coal capacity after 2030** — a core commitment in PDP8.

These targets create a compressed timeline: the transition from a coal-heavy system to a predominantly renewable one must happen within a decade for the 2030 milestones.

---

## Power Development Plan 8 (PDP8) as the model baseline

PDP8 is Vietnam's official long-term electricity planning document. In the model, **PDP8 serves as the reference (baseline) scenario** — the trajectory the economy follows if current energy and climate policies are maintained.

Key PDP8 features modeled:

- Rapid build-out of solar PV, wind (onshore and offshore), and storage
- Phase-down of coal generation capacity
- Large grid investment requirements, particularly for transmission and grid balancing
- Continued economic growth at 6–7% per year through the 2030s

PDP8 is ambitious relative to current implementation capacity. The model treats it as a planning baseline, not a certainty. Sensitivity scenarios explore what happens when specific elements of PDP8 are delayed or underfunded.

---

## Investment scale

The IWH Investment Needs Assessment (2025) estimates that **meeting PDP8 targets requires cumulative investment of approximately USD 136 billion** between 2026 and 2050 in the energy sector alone. This breaks down across:

- Renewable energy capacity (solar, wind, storage)
- Grid reinforcement and transmission infrastructure
- Energy efficiency in industry, services, and households
- Adaptation measures for climate-vulnerable assets

This is a large number relative to Vietnam's current GDP and public fiscal space. The key economic question is not whether Vietnam can eventually afford this — it can — but whether the **timing, financing cost, and institutional capacity** are adequate to execute it at the pace PDP8 requires.

---

## The financing gap

The IWH Financial Assessment (2025) identifies a clear financing bottleneck in the **pre-2035 window**. The combination of:

1. Large upfront investment requirements (especially grid and storage)
2. High cost of capital (market borrowing costs of 8–10% for private energy projects)
3. Limited domestic institutional investor base
4. Uncertainty over carbon market revenue streams

...creates conditions in which PDP8 investment can be delayed or scaled back, causing cost and emissions divergence from the plan.

**Green finance instruments** — concessional loans, blended finance structures, green bonds, and international development bank facilities — are designed to address this gap by lowering the effective cost of capital. The model's finance scenarios (GF_A, GF_B, GF_C) quantify how large these effects are in macroeconomic terms.

---

## Carbon market development

Vietnam is developing its domestic emissions trading system (ETS). A pilot program is under preparation for the power sector and is expected to expand to other covered sectors. Key design questions include:

- **Coverage rate:** What share of emissions are subject to permit requirements?
- **Cap path:** How fast does the total permit supply decline?
- **Revenue recycling:** How are auction revenues spent (budget, subsidies, household transfers)?

The model's Net-Zero (NZ) scenario activates a fully operational ETS with an emissions cap consistent with net-zero by 2050. The ETS scenarios explore how carbon price trajectories change as coverage, efficiency, and financing conditions change.

---

## Why a macroeconomic model is needed

Project-level investment accounting (bottom-up energy models, LCOE analysis) is essential for technology selection and dispatch planning, but it cannot answer the macroeconomic questions:

- How does a USD 136 billion investment program affect GDP growth, household consumption, and sectoral employment over 25 years?
- Does higher efficiency reduce the financing burden or just shift it?
- What is the fiscal cost of carbon revenue recycling to households?
- Do concessional finance instruments actually improve macro outcomes, or do they just redistribute cost?

DGE-METRIC addresses these questions by embedding the energy transition in a full economy model where investment in one sector competes with consumption and investment in others, wages adjust, and households are forward-looking.
